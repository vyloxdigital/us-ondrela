The mechanism

How Ondrela works with your phone

Worth two minutes before you buy, because it explains both what the card is very good at and where it has nothing to offer.

A card like this does not contain a GPS receiver and does not have a mobile data connection of its own. What it does is broadcast a short-range radio signal. Your own phone picks that up when you are near it, which is how ringing and the leave-behind alert work. When the card is somewhere else entirely, it relies on other people's phones quietly passing its position back to the network it belongs to.

That second mechanism is the whole trick, and it has one consequence worth sitting with: the card is only findable where other compatible phones go. In a busy city it will be found quickly. On an empty rural road, in a cargo hold or inside a metal container, it may report nothing for a long time, because nothing is there to overhear it.

The clearest claim on the whole listing, and the one thing that distinguishes this card from most of its rivals.

Six months per charge is the published figure, restored by dropping the card onto any standard wireless charging pad. That is a genuinely useful design decision. Important

Ondrela, privacy and unwanted tracking

Any tracker small enough to hide in a wallet is small enough to hide in somebody else's bag. Both phone platforms now build in defences, and this is the one section worth reading twice.

The official Ondrela listing describes location data as encrypted and visible only to the owner. The other half of that picture is handled by the phone platform rather than the seller, and it is the half most buyers never read about: what happens when a tracker is travelling with somebody who did not put it there. Both Apple and Google have built defences for exactly that, and they apply to any card you buy.

What the platforms do about it

In 2023 Apple and Google jointly submitted an industry specification which, in Apple's own words, will allow Bluetooth location-tracking devices to be compatible with unauthorized tracking detection and alerts on both platforms. The same announcement acknowledges plainly that trackers "can also be misused for unwanted tracking of individuals". Samsung, Tile, Chipolo, eufy Security and Pebblebee were named as supporting the draft.

If your phone warns you about an unknown tracker

Apple's personal safety guide tells iPhone users to tap the notification, then tap Play Sound or, where available, Find Nearby. Google's guidance for Android is the same shape: play a sound to locate it, then save the tracker's information. Both are explicit about the next step if the device cannot be found — Apple says to go to a safe public location and contact law enforcement, and Google says to go to a public location and contact law enforcement or a trusted contact.

The limits worth knowing

These alerts are not instant and they are not perfect. Google notes that its system sends at most one alert per tracker per day and weighs several factors before firing, so a device may travel with somebody for a while before anything appears. Apple's coverage of third-party trackers that follow the joint specification requires a recent iOS version. A reasonable summary is that the protection is real, improving, and not something to rely on absolutely.

Important

Using Ondrela lawfully

A tracker is for your property. Putting one on a person, or on property that is not yours, is a different act entirely.

The Department of Justice's Office on Violence Against Women defines stalking as engaging in a course of conduct directed at a specific person that would cause a reasonable person to fear for their safety or to suffer substantial emotional distress. Using a location device to follow somebody without their knowledge can fall squarely inside that description, and state law adds its own offences on top. If what you are weighing up involves another adult's movements rather than your own luggage, the answer is no, and it is not a close call.

If you think you are being tracked

Take it seriously and get help from people who do this for a living. For immediate danger the Office on Violence Against Women says to call 911. The Federal Trade Commission's guidance on monitoring technology recommends contacting a domestic violence advocate before removing anything, since cutting off access can escalate the situation, and advises using a different device to make that contact. It lists the National Domestic Violence Hotline on 1-800-799-7233, with live chat at thehotline.org.

The Office on Violence Against Women also points to the National Center for Victims of Crime on 855-484-2846 and the StrongHearts Native Helpline on 844-762-8483.

None of that is a reason to avoid buying a tracker for your own belongings, which is what the overwhelming majority of them are used for. It is here because a page selling one should say it, and because the official listing does not.

Expectations

What to expect from Ondrela

Five things worth knowing about every card tracker, so the one you buy does the job you bought it for.

  • It finds things, rather than navigating to them. There is no satellite receiver in a card this thin. Position comes from nearby phones, which is why it is superb in a city, an airport or an office and quiet on an empty moor.
  • It needs people around to report in. In a sealed hold or a shipping container the card waits until it is near handsets again, then catches up. Expect a last-known position rather than a live trail in those places.
  • It shows you where, and you decide what next. If something ends up somewhere it should not be, both Apple and Google say the same thing: do not go and collect it yourself, contact law enforcement.
  • It makes theft recoverable, not impossible. A card in a wallet tells you where the wallet went. A lock is still a lock, and a tracker is not one.
  • It belongs on your things, not on people. Covered in the two sections above, and the one rule worth taking seriously.

The seller claims none of these things, to its credit. They are here because the category attracts the assumptions, and because somebody buying their first tracker should know exactly what arrives.

  • Rechargeable here, rather than disposable at end of life
  • Cannot clip to a keyring without a pouch
  • Larger surface area, so it is easier to spot and remove
  • Thin bodies leave less room for a loud speaker

The keyring shape

Plain English

Ondrela terms explained

Eight expressions you will meet on the listing or in your phone's settings.

Finding network
The crowd of phones that quietly relay a tracker's position back to its owner. Apple and Google each run one, and a tracker normally belongs to a single network.
Leave-behind alert
A notification when you move away from a tracked item. It fires on your own phone noticing the signal has gone, not on the card doing anything clever.
Unknown tracker alert
The reverse feature: your phone warning you that somebody else's tracker appears to be travelling with you. Built into both iOS and Android.
Water resistant
A weaker claim than waterproof, and meaningless without a rating. Splash survival and submersion are different things.
Ingress protection rating
The standard two-digit code, such as IP67, that grades dust and water resistance. Worth knowing as the difference between water resistant and waterproof.
Wireless charging pad
The same inductive pad used for phones and earbuds. The card is said to charge on any standard one, which means no proprietary cable to lose.
Up to
A ceiling rather than a flat rate. Ondrela's "up to 70%" is reached by the four-card bundle; a single card is 46% off.
Reference price
The single-card figure every discount is calculated against. Ondrela's is $82.99, which is where 46%, 58%, 64% and 70% all come from.
